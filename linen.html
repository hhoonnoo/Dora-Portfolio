Case Study

Linen

An AI-powered fashion app offering fashion advice, outfit generation based on preferences, virtual try-on, and an Instagram-style interface for fashion inspiration.

Tools Figma, Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator
Timeline Oct 2023 – Jan 2024
Role Product Design & Art Direction
Collaborator Chisom M.
Context Senior Capstone Project

Linen is a senior capstone project that reimagines personal styling through AI — giving every college student a smart, budget-conscious fashion assistant in their pocket.

View Prototype

The Problem

What's standing between college students and their best look?

Through user research interviews with 18–25 year-olds, we identified three recurring pain points that made getting dressed every day more stressful than it needed to be.

01

Outfit Selection Difficulty

Choosing an outfit takes too much time and mental energy, especially when balancing a busy academic schedule.

02

Financial Constraints

Limited budgets make it hard to explore new styles or buy pieces that truly work together — leading to underused wardrobes.

03

Trend Awareness Gaps

Choosing the right outfit for the right occasion — interviews, dates, social events — is a persistent source of anxiety.

Solution

Four features that address every pain point

01

AI-Generated Outfits

Personalized outfit recommendations generated by AI based on occasions, weather, and user preferences — removing the guesswork entirely.

02

3D Digitization & Virtual Try-On

Digitize wardrobe items and virtually try on outfits before getting dressed, enabling risk-free wardrobe exploration without buying anything new.

03

Community Inspiration Feed

An Instagram-style feed of user-generated outfits from around the world, bridging the trend awareness gap through social discovery.

04

AR Try-On with In-App Shopping

Try on items from the explore feed using augmented reality, then add to wishlist or purchase directly — without leaving the app.

01 — Research

Understanding the user

We conducted five one-on-one user interviews with college students between ages 18–25. Participants were recruited from campus and screened to ensure they actively engaged with fashion decisions on a daily basis.

Core Pain Points

Key Findings

  • Getting dressed takes too long — participants reported spending 15–30 minutes daily on outfit decisions.
  • Budget limits exploration — students feel stuck wearing the same items repeatedly due to financial constraints.
  • Occasion anxiety — choosing the right outfit for specific social contexts causes significant stress.

Competitive Analysis

Nike Virtual View

Strength

Polished AR try-on experience with strong brand recognition and seamless shopping integration.

Weakness

Limited to Nike products only — no support for users' existing wardrobes or third-party items.

Acloset

Strength

Digital wardrobe organization with outfit logging and statistics — great for closet management.

Weakness

No AI generation or virtual try-on; heavily manual input required with no social discovery layer.

Aiuta

Strength

Virtual try-on using AI photo fitting; supports multiple retailers with a clean UI.

Weakness

No community or inspiration features; purely transactional with limited personalization over time.

Insight: Grouping information together using cards can best utilize space. Familiar interface patterns enable quick user adoption, and user-generated content drives sustained engagement.

02 — Ideation

From sketches to structure

We began with low-fidelity sketches and peer collaboration sessions to explore a wide range of interaction models. Ideas were stress-tested against our research findings before narrowing down to a core user flow.

The user flow was mapped across five key pages — covering the complete journey from onboarding through AI generation, virtual try-on, community browsing, and closet management.

03 — Design

Wireframes & lo-fi prototype

Low-fidelity wireframes were created in Figma and tested with real users before any visual styling was applied. This ensured that core interaction patterns and information hierarchy were sound before investing in high-fidelity execution.

Lo-fi prototype testing revealed that users expected the AI generator to surface on the home screen — not buried in a secondary tab — and that the closet management flow needed to be reordered to match mental models around getting dressed.

04 — User Testing

1:1 moderated usability sessions

Three participants completed moderated usability sessions — conducted both in-person and remotely via Zoom. Tasks covered the full product journey: onboarding, outfit generation, closet management, and social browsing.

UI

Typography sizing on secondary screens was too small for quick scanning. Increased base font sizes and improved contrast ratios across all flows.

Explore Page

Users wanted to filter the community feed by occasion and style category — not just by trending. Added filter chips to the top of the feed.

Closet Page

The add-item flow required too many steps. Streamlined to a 2-tap camera capture that auto-categorizes garments using AI tagging.

AI Generator

Users wanted more control over regenerating specific parts of an outfit rather than the whole look. Introduced per-item swap functionality.

05 — Hi-fi Prototype

The final design system & interactive prototype

The high-fidelity prototype brings together the full design system — typography, color, component library, and micro-interactions — into a clickable, testable product. Every screen was built in Figma with a shared component library to ensure consistency across the five core flows.

The prototype was presented at the senior capstone showcase and received feedback from peers, faculty, and industry professionals.

06 — Reflection

What I learned

Linen was my most research-intensive project to date. Managing a full UX process — from interviews through hi-fi delivery — within a four-month timeline pushed me to prioritize ruthlessly and stay grounded in user needs rather than design preferences.

"

User testing is key to UX design. You can sketch the most elegant flow imaginable and still get it completely wrong — the moment a real user sits down with your prototype, you learn more in five minutes than in five weeks of self-critique.
